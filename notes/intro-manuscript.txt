INTRODUCTION MANUSCRIPT (slides 1-5, about 3.5 minutes)

Spoken script for the opening of the talk: where Raft came from, what was wrong
with Paxos, and how that shaped Raft. Sources are sections 1, 3 and 4 of the
paper (Ongaro & Ousterhout, In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm,
extended version, 2014) and section 5.2 for the election-timeout choice.
The same text is in site/notes.js, so it shows in the speaker view.


SLIDE 1  Title                                                      (~10 s)

We chose the Raft paper. To understand why Raft looks the way it does, you have
to start with what came before it.


SLIDE 2  Consensus, and the algorithm everyone used                 (~50 s)

[click] The problem the paper attacks is consensus: a group of machines that acts as one
reliable machine, and keeps working when some of them fail. Every replicated
database and configuration store needs this.

[click] The standard answer was Paxos. Leslie Lamport wrote it around 1989, and
for the ten years before this paper it was, in the authors' words, almost
synonymous with consensus. It is what courses taught, and most implementations
started from it or were influenced by it.

[click] And for good reasons. Paxos is proven safe, it keeps working as long as
a majority of the servers are up, so five servers survive two failures, and in
the normal case it is efficient.

[click] The catch is what Paxos actually is at its core. Single-decree Paxos
agrees on one value. To get a log, you run one instance per entry and glue the
instances together. That is multi-Paxos, and the gluing is where the trouble
starts.


SLIDE 3  What's wrong with Paxos?                                   (~70 s)

The authors see two drawbacks, and the paper is a reaction to both.

[click] First, Paxos is exceptionally hard to understand. Lamport's original
paper is famously opaque. Several simplified explanations exist, but they only
cover the single-decree part, and they are still hard. The authors did an
informal survey at NSDI 2012 and found few people who were comfortable with
Paxos, even among experienced researchers. They themselves needed almost a
year, and only got there by designing their own alternative.

[click] Their diagnosis is the foundation. Single-decree Paxos has two stages
that have no simple intuitive explanation and cannot be understood
independently. Then multi-Paxos adds composition rules on top, with more
subtlety.

[click] Second, Paxos is a poor foundation for real systems. There is no
agreed-upon multi-Paxos. Lamport mostly described the single-decree protocol
and sketched the rest, so many details are missing. The architecture also
works against you: choosing log entries independently and then merging them
into a sequence just adds complexity, and the symmetric peer-to-peer core
makes sense for one decision, but for a series of decisions it is simpler and
faster to elect a leader first.

[click] So every real implementation starts with Paxos, hits these gaps, and
ends up with a significantly different architecture. That is slow and
error-prone, and it means the correctness proofs no longer apply to the
system you actually built. Google's Chubby team said exactly this. (Point at
the quote.)


SLIDE 4  Understandability as the primary goal                      (~35 s)

That is the motivation. The research question follows directly from it:
can we define a consensus algorithm for practical systems and describe it in a
way that is significantly easier to learn than Paxos?

[click] And not just an algorithm that works. In their words, it had to be
obvious why it works, because system builders need those intuitions.

[click] Understandability became the test for every design decision. Where
they had alternatives, they asked: how hard is this to explain? How complex is
its state space? Does it have subtle implications? They admit this is
subjective, which is why they later measure it with a user study.

[click] The key results up front: 43 students learned both algorithms, and 33
did better on Raft. Raft is formally specified and proven safe. And it is as
efficient as Paxos. We come back to all three.


SLIDE 5  How that shaped Raft                                       (~60 s)

Two techniques turned that goal into a design.

[click] Decomposition. Where Paxos's two stages cannot be understood
separately, Raft splits consensus into pieces that can: leader election, log
replication, safety, and membership changes. (Point at the three pillars.)
You can learn each one on its own, and that is how we present it today.

[click] Reducing the state space: fewer ways for servers to disagree, so fewer
cases to think about. Three examples, each one a direct answer to Paxos.
(Point at the table.)

[click] A strong leader. Log entries only flow from the leader to the
followers. Paxos has symmetric peers and only suggests a leader as an
optimization.

[click] Logs without holes, appended in order, and only a few allowed ways for
two logs to differ. Paxos picks each log slot independently and merges
afterwards.

[click] Randomness where it simplifies. Election timeouts are random: "choose
any, it doesn't matter". They considered ranking candidates instead, and
dropped it because it had subtle availability problems.

[click] That is my part. From here on the simulator does the talking: we
rebuild Raft from nothing, one failure at a time. Every rule we add is from
the paper.
